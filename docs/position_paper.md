## When to Intervene: AARC-Grounded Friction Signals for Training AI Pre-Mediation Agents

### Introduction

Conflict resolution research has long established that people embroiled in interpersonal dispute rarely arrive at the negotiating table — or the chat interface — in a state of epistemic readiness. They arrive with a position already formed, a proposed solution already in mind, and an emotional activation level that makes it difficult to evaluate perspectives other than their own. What they are actually seeking, in many cases, is not a solution but a first step toward one: some form of acknowledgement that their experience of the situation is real and that their standing in it is legitimate. This is not irrational; it is, as mediation literature has documented, the necessary precondition for productive substantive engagement. The problem is that people often do not know this is what they need, and they do not ask for it in those terms.

Current AI systems respond to the question that is asked — the practical one — and in doing so they tend to validate. This is not a neutral act. A system trained on human approval learns, reliably, to confirm rather than to challenge, to endorse the user's frame rather than to interrogate it. The person's emotions feel acknowledged; their proposed approach feels endorsed. Both problems appear to have been addressed. But undiscriminating validation is not the same as useful acknowledgement. It reinforces the user's existing frame, leaves the other party's perspective unconsidered, and returns the person to the conflict more entrenched — and potentially more confident in an approach that may not serve them.

The failure described above is not simply a matter of model behaviour. It reflects a deeper mismatch between the kind of response a content-oriented system is capable of producing and the psychological state of the person receiving it.

Research on physiological arousal in interpersonal conflict establishes that as emotional activation mounts, higher-order cognitive function degrades in a measurable and systematic way. Gottman and Levenson (1992) find that when autonomic arousal during conflict exceeds a threshold they term *diffuse physiological arousal*, parties lose the capacity to process their counterpart's communication and default to withdrawal and entrenchment rather than engagement. Arnsten (2009) supplies the neurobiological mechanism: acute stress triggers catecholamine release in the prefrontal cortex that impairs working memory, cognitive flexibility, and attentional regulation, while simultaneously strengthening amygdala activation — rapidly shifting the brain from reflective to reflexive control. A person in this state is neurologically ill-equipped to evaluate a content-level correction, however accurate, that requires them to revise their model of the situation. Delivering one is not merely insufficient; it may deepen the deficit it cannot detect, by being experienced not as clarification but as challenge.

This is the computational instantiation of a failure mode that dispute resolution theory has long named: the *content trap* — the tendency to address only the propositional surface of a dispute while its relational, procedural, emotional, and identity dimensions remain unresolved. A system that responds to *what should I do here?* with an answer to that question, when the prior condition for receiving that answer has not yet been established, has fallen into the content trap regardless of how accurate the answer is. Correcting for this requires more than better calibration of the response. It requires an architecture capable of detecting what kind of intervention is appropriate at a given moment in a dialogue — and withholding content-level correction until the conditions that make it productive are present.

The limitation is not incidental to current AI systems — it reflects a structural characteristic of the computational frameworks that underlie them. Computational approaches to conflict assistance are nascent: structured platforms for AI-assisted negotiation have emerged, but these are largely confined to closed-domain, single-encounter bargaining tasks — price negotiation, resource allocation, and customer-service dispute resolution — that do not engage the ongoing relational dynamics interpersonal conflict requires (Chawla et al., 2021; Hale et al., 2025). Recent work applying large language models and reinforcement learning to dialogue friction and disagreement has focused primarily on AI-human interaction (Ahmad et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024), leaving the analogous challenge of AI-assisted conflict support largely unaddressed. Koçak et al. (2025) provide initial evidence of a deeper skew: LLMs deployed in conflict diagnosis tasks tend to focus on causal and factual aspects of disagreement rather than moral and evaluative ones — suggesting this tendency is not a design choice unique to any one system but a characteristic of current LLM-based conflict analysis. In parallel, a mature body of work in the conflict resolution and negotiation fields has documented the dynamics of human-human conflict and the conditions under which mediation succeeds (Fisher & Ury, 1981; Wilmot & Hocker, 1997; Jennings et al., 2001). What remains largely unaddressed is the space between these two streams: how the computational techniques developed for AI-human dialogue management can be extended and grounded in the full-dimensional theory of interpersonal conflict that the mediation literature prescribes — and in doing so, corrected for the content trap they currently replicate.

FPO (Frictive Policy Optimization), one such computational dialogue management system, reframes alignment as a problem of epistemic control: rather than optimizing for surface-level helpfulness or compliance with stated preferences, it asks when and how an agent should intervene to regulate the quality of belief evolving over a dialogue trajectory (Pustejovsky & Krishnaswamy, 2025). At its core is a structured friction functional that decomposes epistemic risk into five measurable components: uncertainty miscalibration (*Unc*), logical contradiction (*Contr*; Bowman et al., 2015; Nie et al., 2021), safety hazard (*Haz*), value conflict (*ValConf*), and expected information gain (*InfoGain*). The first four constitute negative friction — epistemic failure modes that degrade belief quality over time; the fifth constitutes positive friction — interventions that incur short-term interaction cost in order to reduce downstream uncertainty, as in the case of well-timed clarification or verification (Thompson et al., 2025). The net friction functional (*F*⁺ − *F*⁻) serves as the optimization target: an intervention is selected — from a compact taxonomy of clarification, verification, challenge, redirection, and refusal — based on its expected contribution to this signal. This architecture treats dialogue not as a sequence of task completions but as an evolving epistemic state that the agent must actively govern.

In its current form, FPO operates on dyadic dialogue between a human and an LLM assistant, where the primary goal is epistemic correction: identifying and resolving faulty propositional content. I argue, however, that extending this capability to pre-mediation conflict assistance — where the agent works with one activated party, helping them process the conflict and prepare for productive engagement — requires more than epistemic correction. An agent capable of this work must detect not only when a user's beliefs are miscalibrated, but when they are not yet ready to receive calibration at all.

I develop such an architecture. The sections that follow review the conflict resolution and computational literatures and identify the theoretical resources needed to bridge them; introduce the CPRI and AARC frameworks as the diagnostic and decision-making foundation for a turn-level intervention system; propose an annotation schema and corpus construction methodology for generating the reward signal needed to train that system; and outline the training and evaluation approach. The scope is deliberately bounded: the paper addresses the pre-mediation phase rather than live multi-party mediation. This is not a retreat from a harder problem — it is recognition that grounding intervention competence in a tractable, well-defined conversational structure is the necessary first step. The goal is not to replace human mediation but to ask what a computationally grounded pre-mediation assistant would require — and to argue that it requires substantially more than the current state of AI-assisted conflict work provides.

### Conflict Dimensions and the Content Trap: The CPRI Framework

A central insight of dispute resolution theory is that conflict operates simultaneously across multiple dimensions, and that privileging any single dimension risks misdiagnosis of what is actually driving an impasse. The field identifies four goals that parties pursue when in conflict (Wilmot & Hocker, 1997; Ting-Toomey, 1988):

1. Content: the substantive issues in dispute  
2. Relationship: concerning the history of interaction, how parties wish to be treated, and the nature of their interdependence  
3. Process: governing how communication and engagement will proceed  
4. Identity: or face-saving, rooted in culture, norms, and self-conception (Ting-Toomey, 1988)

Crucially, these dimensions do not carry equal weight in all conflicts, nor do they operate independently — relational and identity-based tensions frequently manifest as substantive disagreements, making the surface content of a dispute a poor guide to its underlying drivers. Dispute resolution theory has long recognized that the relational, procedural, and emotional dimensions of conflict are not downstream of the substantive; they are frequently primary (Wilmot & Hocker, 1997).

Practitioner training in the conflict resolution field has formalized this insight through the Content-Process-Relationship (CPR) framework (Wilmot & Hocker, 1997), extended to include an identity dimension (Justice Institute of British Columbia, 2021) — referred to here as the CPRI framework. Failure at any vertex risks escalating negative affect, which can precipitate breakdown and entrench unresolved conflict. This pedagogical structure is explicitly designed to warn practitioners against the content trap.

FPO's friction functional and action space are defined entirely over propositional content: its five components detect epistemic failure modes, and its intervention taxonomy — clarification, verification, challenge, redirection, and refusal (Pustejovsky & Krishnaswamy, 2025) — contains no moves for acknowledgment, face-repair, or process affirmation. It therefore registers no signal for relational rupture, procedural illegitimacy, or identity threat — precisely the dimensions CPRI identifies as primary drivers of impasse. Compounded by the physiological constraints established above, prioritizing epistemic clarity before relational and cooperative conditions are restored is not merely incomplete — it may be actively counterproductive.

An exclusively epistemic approach to friction detection therefore replicates precisely the content trap at the computational level, and correcting for it requires grounding the intervention taxonomy in the full dimensionality that conflict resolution theory prescribes.

### Theoretical Background: CPRI as Diagnosis, AARC as Decision Procedure

While the CPRI framework provides an essential corrective to content-centric thinking, it operates at a broad level of conflict diagnosis rather than intervention design. It identifies that a dispute has a relational or identity dimension, but it does not specify when in a dialogue that dimension becomes acute, which dimension to address when multiple are simultaneously elevated, or what a mediator should do at the turn level when a deficit is detected.

For a computational agent, this is a critical limitation: case-level taxonomy cannot generate a preference signal between competing interventions, nor can it serve as the basis for turn-level annotation without a more fine-grained operationalization. Furthermore, the boundaries between CPRI dimensions are not cleanly separable at the utterance level: relational and identity-based deficits in particular manifest in overlapping ways in dialogue text — face concerns simultaneously engage a party's self-image and their regard for the other, collapsing the identity/relational boundary at the utterance level (Ting-Toomey, 1988) — making reliable annotation difficult without more behaviorally specific targets.

What is needed is a framework that specifies not just what dimensions of conflict exist, but what a deficit in each dimension looks like in dialogue — capturing what a party says, how they respond, and what breaks down — and that maps those deficit states to actionable intervention strategies. The AARC model (Hoben, 2020), drawing on a practitioner lineage that includes foundational work on the role of dignity in conflict resolution (Hicks, 2011; Susskind & Field, 1996), supplies the organizing framework; the operationalization of each component as observable, turn-level dialogue behavior is my contribution. AARC is currently only a practitioner framework developed within the conflict resolution field that has not been formally validated in published empirical research. However, the operationalization of a human conflict-resolution theory as a computational control signal has direct precedent: Shaikh et al. (2024) demonstrate that the Interest-Rights-Power framework can be used to condition LLM outputs in a conflict-training context, producing measurable improvements in users' subsequent conflict behavior. That work is training-oriented — the system teaches humans to manage conflict more effectively — rather than mediation-oriented, and it conditions a single theoretical framework rather than a multi-component deficit-detection schema. I treat that gap as part of my contribution: by operationalizing each AARC component as observable, turn-level dialogue behavior and testing whether the resulting labels produce a discriminative preference signal, I provide initial computational evidence that the framework's components are annotatable and useful as a reward signal. This is a necessary but not sufficient step toward empirical grounding: proxy reward model accuracy does not establish that AARC labels predict mediation outcomes. That gap — from computational utility to construct validity — requires external validation against human mediator judgments or behavioral outcomes in controlled sessions, and is deferred to future work.

## The AARC Framework: Taxonomy and Operationalization**

AARC identifies four components of conflict interaction that a practitioner — or computational agent — must be capable of addressing: Acknowledgement, Agency, Reciprocity, and Clarity. Each component was operationalized through a staged pipeline designed to ground abstract deficit definitions in observable, turn-level dialogue behavior. The theoretical grounding established above was used to prime a retrieval-augmented generation system (Notebook LM), queried against practitioner conflict resolution manuals — a bespoke pipeline rather than an established taxonomy-induction method; structured prompts constrained the output to the four fixed component definitions and required that each deficit signal be mutually exclusive, observable in dialogue text, and mappable to a distinct intervention type. The resulting draft taxonomy was revised by the researcher to resolve ambiguities, adjudicate boundary cases, and finalize the operationalization plan.

The most consequential design decision in the operationalization concerns the direction of attribution. All four deficit labels are speaker-attributed: the annotation question at each turn is not *what deficit is this utterance producing in the other party?* but *what does this utterance reveal about what the speaker themselves is currently lacking or exhibiting?* An Acknowledgement deficit does not indicate that the speaker is failing to recognize the other party; it indicates that the speaker's own turn exhibits an unmet need for recognition. An Agency deficit does not describe a structural constraint imposed from outside; it describes the speaker's exhibited perception of foreclosure. A Reciprocity deficit describes the speaker's absent cooperative orientation, not the impact of their utterance on the listener. A Clarity deficit describes the speaker's own epistemic miscalibration, not confusion produced in the other party. This inversion — from relational effect to individual exhibition — is what makes the schema annotatable at the turn level without requiring inference about listener responses, and it maintains consistency between the annotator's information set and the downstream model's inference-time information set.

The taxonomy for each component is organized into six subsections, each addressing a distinct methodological problem. Deficit signals (§x.1) specify observable behaviors in the speaker's turn, with precision qualifiers distinguishing necessary-but-not-sufficient conditions from high-precision signals sufficient in isolation — a distinction that prevents both under-diagnosis from demanding too many co-occurring signals and over-diagnosis from treating isolated markers as conclusive. Diagnostic lead-up (§x.2) formalizes how the seven-turn context window informs the label: many signals are not diagnosable from a single turn but become reliable as the culmination of an accumulating pattern in the speaker's own prior turns. Turn-zero handling (§x.3) addresses the structural asymmetry at the opening turn, where no lead-up window exists, specifying conservative defaults and the narrow signal classes legible from surface form alone. Resolution indicators (§x.4) distinguish three cases in which a deficit label yields to N: post-repair confirmation, in which a prior deficit was addressed and the speaker's current turn shows positive re-engagement; speaker-as-repairer, in which the speaker's current turn performs a repair action directed at the other party's deficit; and accommodation and withdrawal, in which deficit signals cease without genuine re-engagement — a case that would be misclassified as resolution under a simpler coding scheme, but which instead commonly marks the opening of a new and different deficit trajectory. Boundary cases (§x.5) provide intra-turn and backward-looking discriminators for adjacent components, cross-documented from both directions. Gold standard candidates (§x.6) anchor inter-annotator calibration with worked examples covering deficit trajectories, resolution transitions, and boundary cases.

### Acknowledgement

Acknowledgement deficits ask: does the speaker show signs of needing to be received — not agreed with, but registered as present and heard?

Deficit Signals
 
- **Self-minimizing and self-discounting language** — the speaker hedges the legitimacy of their own concern while still pursuing it in the same turn; preemptive concession that signals an expectation of non-registration ("I know this probably sounds stupid, but it keeps bothering me")
- **Universalizing appeals** — the speaker recruits imagined consensus to validate experience the other party has not yet directly acknowledged ("any reasonable person would feel this way"); typically a second-order move after a direct disclosure has failed to land
- **Grievance cataloguing** — an evidence-marshaling structure in which the speaker enumerates past offenses with escalating specificity, invokes third-party witnesses, and positions the self as wronged; the grammatical subject returns consistently to self-as-injured even when the surface content concerns the other party's conduct

### Agency

Agency deficits ask: does the speaker experience themselves as someone who can still meaningfully participate in shaping what happens here?

Deficit Signals
 
- **Powerless speech markers** — hedging, qualifiers, minimizers, deferential brevity; treated as *necessary but not sufficient*; diagnosable as Agency only in combination with constraint narratives, a progressive deferral pattern in the speaker's own prior turns, or procedural avoidance markers
- **Explicit constraint narratives** — self-report of perceived entrapment ("I feel like I can't move," "I have no voice in this"); high-precision signals sufficient in isolation
- **Procedural avoidance** — resistance expressed as disengagement ("this isn't going to change anything," "I don't have time for this")
- **Nominal, disengaged participation** — flat acknowledgment tokens or monosyllabic responses co-occurring with powerless speech or constraint language

### Reciprocity 

Reciprocity deficits ask: does the speaker treat this interaction as a joint process, or are they advancing a fixed outcome or undermining the other party's standing? 

Positional and extractive signals indicate the speaker is treating the interaction as zero-sum and advancing toward a fixed outcome:
 
- **Positional language** — both hard directive forms ("I refuse to," "you have to") and soft forms that present desired outcomes as settled rather than open
- **Absence of interest exploration** — in combination with positional holding
- **Stockpiling of past grievances** — deployed as leverage; distinguished from Acknowledgement's grievance cataloguing by whether the list serves a positional argument or a recognition bid
- **Volitional withholding of information** — detectable when the speaker deflects direct requests for information they demonstrably possess
- **Strategic conciliation** — surface agreement performed without cooperative substance

Other-directed attack and dismissal signals indicate active undermining of the other party's standing as a collaborator:
 
- **Minimizing and dismissing** — directed at the other party
- **Moralizing from a high-ground posture** — deploys a moral frame to attack the other's standing; structurally identical to Acknowledgement's universalizing appeals, but directed outward rather than inward
- **Other-attacking blame** — organized around the other party's deficiencies
- **Non-responsive counter-attack** — speaker pivots to a new grievance rather than engaging the other party's prior content
- **Sustained deflection** — without accompanying powerless cues

### Clarity

Clarity deficits ask: does the speaker proceed as though something is shared or settled between the parties when the interaction hasn't actually established that? Deficits fall into two clusters based on direction of miscalibration.

Commitment-Without-Warrant (Overcertainty)

- **Unverified intent attribution** — treating another party's motive as settled without confirmation
- **Overcertainty on underspecified matters** — confidence exceeds what the exchange has verified
- **Inference overshoot** — conclusion has climbed the Ladder of Inference (observation → meaning → evaluative judgment) without verification at any step; includes reading current events through the lens of old grievances

Inference overshoot names the cumulative case: the speaker's conclusion has advanced from observation, through added meaning, to generalized evaluative judgment — ascending the Ladder of Inference (Argyris, 1982; Ross, 1994) — without verification at any intermediate step; this inferential trajectory includes attributional lensing, in which current events are interpreted through the distorting lens of prior grievances rather than evaluated on their own terms (Sillars, 1980; Bradbury & Fincham, 1990).

Insufficient Specification (Undercertainty / Vagueness)

- **Vague language** — persists despite the other party's attempts to engage concretely
- **Self-contradiction across the window** — speaker's current claim conflicts with their own earlier claim, unacknowledged
- **Incongruence within a turn** — stated content contradicts the speaker's own non-verbal or paraverbal signals


With each component defined in terms of observable deficit states, the question is how they are grounded in CPRI's broader diagnostic categories. Acknowledgement addresses relational and identity deficits — both require a party to feel recognized and their standing in the interaction affirmed; Agency addresses identity and process deficits — both concern whether a party can meaningfully participate in and influence the exchange; Reciprocity addresses process and relational deficits — both depend on mutual commitment and equitable engagement; and Clarity addresses content deficits, corresponding directly to FPO's existing epistemic scope.

The ordering of AARC's components carries diagnostic significance. AARC does not prescribe a fixed procedural sequence; rather, it implies a diagnostic priority: deficits at lower levels warrant attention before content-level correction is likely to be effective. This priority ordering converges with findings from several independent scholarly traditions. Pruitt's (2015) readiness theory holds that productive negotiation requires two concurrent perceptions: motivation to pursue settlement, and optimism that the counterpart will reciprocate conciliatory moves. It is the second condition — optimism about reciprocation — that corresponds to AARC's Reciprocity level. Procedural justice research establishes that disputants' willingness to engage constructively is determined by their perception of *process control* — the belief that they can meaningfully present their case and influence the procedure — independent of outcome favorability (Thibaut & Walker, 1975); and that authentic cooperative engagement presupposes that parties perceive themselves as neither helpless nor powerless (Deutsch, 1973). Together these ground the condition AARC names Agency. And the dignity literature establishes acknowledgement as a precondition for constructive engagement (Hicks, 2011; Susskind & Field, 1996) — the relational and identity groundwork without which the transition from positional to interest-level dialogue (Fisher & Ury, 1981) cannot occur. AARC's first three components, taken together, correspond to a set of preconditions for productive substantive engagement that converge across these independent traditions.

Clarity is the level at which content correction becomes productive only once those preconditions obtain. But when Acknowledgement, Agency, or Reciprocity deficits are simultaneously present, content-level correction may be received not as clarification but as challenge — potentially deepening the deficits it cannot detect. Whether and how this diagnostic priority should be encoded in the reward signal architecture — and whether it can emerge from corpus structure alone or requires explicit operationalization — is an open question deferred to future work. The full intervention taxonomy — specifying for each component the complete set of labeled intervention types, boundary case resolutions, and gold standard dialogue examples — is detailed in the companion annotation schema document.

## Training Methodology

The training framework is designed for deployment in the pre-mediation context: a one-on-one conversation in which an AI assistant works with one party prior to any joint session with the counterpart. In that setting, the agent must detect AARC deficit signals in the party's speech — positional framing, dismissive attribution, Clarity gaps — and apply the corresponding interventions. The training corpus, however, consists of two-party conflict dialogues rather than one-on-one AI conversations. This is a deliberate choice: including AI assistant turns in the corpus would risk signal pollution, since the same model family generating the corpus would evaluate its own outputs at annotation time. The transfer from two-party training to one-party deployment is justified because the linguistic markers of conflict activation — positional language, repetition under affect, unverified inference of intent — are intra-party signals that accumulate in the party's speech regardless of whether their interlocutor is another activated human or a neutral AI. Some deficit signals that are inherently interactional in two-party dialogue may require reinterpretation in the one-on-one setting. This reinterpretation is noted as a limitation and a target for future validation against pre-mediation transcripts.

The methodology proceeds in six stages, from corpus construction through ablation evaluation.

*Corpus construction* generates a set of 80–100 two-party conflict dialogues through the persona-scenario pipeline described in the following section. Each dialogue pairs two character personas with a conflict brief using a two-agent architecture in which each generating instance is restricted to its own profile. A director monitors each turn for character drift; a moderator governs termination. Dialogues run to a minimum of eight and a maximum of twenty turns.

*LLM pre-annotation* applies the AARC deficit schema to every turn in the corpus, producing a draft label across all four components and a brief rationale. These labels are provisional: they serve as a structured starting point for human review and carry no evidential weight about the schema's validity or the corpus's annotation distribution.

*Human gold annotation* subjects a researcher-selected subset of turns to review, correcting and confirming LLM pre-labels to produce authoritative gold labels. Inter-annotator reliability across the four binary components is assessed using Cohen's kappa, Krippendorff's alpha, and per-component F1 score; the gold standard is finalized before silver annotation scaling begins.

*Silver annotation scaling* extends deficit labeling to the full corpus using Claude Sonnet-4-6, validated against the gold standard at mean κ = 0.671. This annotator is a distinct component from the downstream reward model: its task is to produce turn-level deficit labels, not to score interventions. Silver label quality is monitored through periodic comparison against the gold set using per-component F1 score, with consistency filtering applied.

*Preference pair construction* operates on the silver-annotated corpus. High-friction windows — seven-turn sliding windows containing at least one deficit-positive turn — constitute candidate intervention points. At each, N candidate interventions are generated from the component-appropriate types, scored across all four AARC components, and group-ranked by aggregate score; contrastive pairs are sampled from each ranking, targeting a preference corpus of 5,000–20,000 pairs.

*Reward model training and ablation* fine-tunes a LLaMA 8B base model on the preference corpus using the Bradley-Terry framework. A held-out agreement threshold of 70% serves as the quality gate before evaluation. The central empirical question — whether AARC deficit labels produce a richer reward signal than FPO friction scores alone — is addressed through an ablation study comparing the AARC-enriched model against a friction-only baseline trained on preference pairs derived from the raw friction functional (*F*⁺ − *F*⁻) without AARC labels. Policy training — fine-tuning against the reward model using group relative policy optimization — is the natural downstream application of this work and is deferred to future work.

### Corpus Construction

The corpus is constructed in two phases, both drawing on a persona-scenario pipeline that conditions dialogue generation on structured character profiles and conflict briefs rather than on AARC deficit labels directly. This design addresses two distinct validity concerns. Constructing dialogues by prompting for deficit categories directly — "write a dialogue showing an Acknowledgement deficit" — risks producing stylized instantiations that are too uniform to serve as reliable annotation targets; such a corpus reflects the framework's vocabulary rather than the naturalistic distribution of conflict behavior the framework is intended to detect. Equally, deficit language present in the generation context is likely to influence subsequent LLM annotation, producing circularity rather than independent labeling. The persona-scenario approach prevents both: deficit patterns are allowed to emerge from character psychology interacting with situational stakes, rather than being specified as generation targets. This strategy has direct precedent in DiaSynth (Suresh et al., 2025), a synthetic corpus generation system that conditions LLM-generated dialogue on structured personas and conversational scenarios.

Dialogues are generated by pairing two character personas with a scenario brief using a two-agent architecture: each LLM instance is provided with its assigned character profile and the shared scenario brief, but not with the opposing character's profile. This asymmetry of information is a critical design choice. A single LLM generating both sides of a dialogue has access to both parties' goals simultaneously, which tends to produce cooperative, on-rails exchanges in which misunderstandings resolve readily and conflict escalates by design rather than through genuine goal incompatibility. Restricting each instance to its own persona replicates the information asymmetry characteristic of real conflict — each party is genuinely unaware of the other's internal state — and allows misunderstandings, positional entrenchment, and deficit accumulation to arise from character psychology interacting with situational pressure, rather than from scripted construction.

Generation uses chain-of-thought prompting following DiaSynth (Suresh et al., 2025): for each dialogue turn, the generating instance first reasons through the character's current activation state given the preceding exchange, their behavioral pattern under activation, and the structural pressures specified in the scenario brief, before producing the turn text. This intermediate reasoning step ensures that deficit-relevant behavior accumulates across turns as a pattern — Acknowledgement failures through sustained dismissal, Agency constrictions through language of constraint, Reciprocity breakdowns through positional entrenchment, Clarity gaps through unverified attribution — rather than appearing as designed single-turn instantiations. Dialogue length is governed by a separate moderator check applied after each turn: either a rule-based condition (turn limit reached, or explicit goal achievement detected in the exchange) or a dedicated LLM call that judges whether natural closure has been reached — resolution, genuine impasse, or conversational breakdown. Dialogues are generated to a minimum of eight turns to ensure sufficient coverage under the seven-turn sliding window, and a maximum of twenty turns.

Both design choices operate against the same underlying tendency: LLMs trained with reinforcement learning from human feedback exhibit a strong prior toward cooperative, de-escalating behavior. Instructed to enact conflict, they tend to introduce conciliatory moves and resolve misunderstandings more readily than any activated party would. Information asymmetry is a necessary condition for conflict emergence, not a sufficient one against this prior. Generation is therefore framed as a scripted dramatic exercise: each instance is cast as an actor performing a character in a scene, rather than producing a helpful output — relocating the performance standard from usefulness to character fidelity. This framing introduces a necessary architectural complement: a director, a monitor that does not participate in the dialogue but evaluates each generated turn against the character's declared profile and flags drift toward cooperative defaults before it propagates across the window.

DiaSynth's persona schema centers on demographic and contextual attributes — role, background, and communication style — that condition conversational register without specifying a character's conflict psychology. This is sufficient for producing naturalistic-sounding dialogues, as Suresh et al. (2025) demonstrate: LLM-generated synthetic dialogues conditioned on simulated personas and scenarios capture 90.48% of the performance distribution of in-domain naturalistic data. It is insufficient for producing dialogues in which deficit patterns accumulate with enough psychological coherence to serve as reliable annotation targets. A persona whose conflict behavior is theoretically unconstrained will tend to reproduce the cooperative, resolution-oriented response patterns characteristic of RLHF-trained generators regardless of the antagonistic setup: the drama framing establishes the right task orientation, but cannot substitute for a character whose psychology constrains what in-character behavior looks like.

The persona library consists of 15 character profiles, each grounded in nine dimensions. *Core needs* specify what the character requires from the interaction to remain engaged — the threshold below which productive exchange breaks down. *Beliefs about self* and *beliefs about others* operationalize Bowlby's (1973) internal working models: the character's self-conception as it governs conflict behavior and their default orientation toward the counterpart. These paired representations jointly shape how a persona reads an ambiguous move — whether a counterpart's silence is experienced as withdrawal, indifference, or restraint is determined by the persona's working model of others, not by the surface content of the turn. *Face salience*, following Ting-Toomey's (1988) face-negotiation theory, specifies the degree to which the persona experiences concession, correction, or perceived status asymmetry as threats to identity rather than as content-level moves; it governs whether a given exchange moment registers for the character as a matter of substance or of standing. Each persona is additionally assigned a primary conflict-handling mode from Thomas's (1976) assertiveness × cooperativeness taxonomy — competing, avoiding, collaborating, compromising, or accommodating — which specifies behavioral orientation under pressure and constrains the range of moves the persona will credibly produce. A *behavioral pattern under activation* specifies the characteristic strategy the persona deploys when core needs are threatened; and an *activation profile* and *storm shift*, following Pruitt and Rubin's (1986) account of individual escalation trajectories, specify the conditions under which light tactics give way to heavier ones and the direction of that shift — toward increased intensity, toward withdrawal, or toward generalized grievance. A *repair orientation* specifies the character's receptivity to de-escalation attempts and the conditions under which a counterparty's bid registers as genuine rather than as a further provocation — the form that repair must take to be effective for this persona. Profiles were constructed through structured prompting against the conflict resolution and social cognition literatures without naming AARC components in the generation prompts, to prevent persona vocabulary from anticipating the annotation targets.

Together these nine dimensions constitute a character script in the sense of Schank and Abelson (1977): a structured set of role behaviors, interpretive defaults, and expected transitions that specify how the persona will behave across the range of situational triggers the scenario produces. Treating the persona as a script makes the director's task tractable: rather than evaluating whether a turn is broadly coherent, the director checks whether it is consistent with the character's declared working models, TKI mode, escalation trajectory, face salience, and repair orientation. This two-level architecture — persona-playing generators producing in-character turns, a director-level monitor enforcing profile fidelity — has direct computational precedent in Riedl and Young's (2010) IPOCL narrative planner, which demonstrates that explicitly balancing character-level intentionality against author-level coherence produces more comprehensible and believable character behavior than single-level generation; the director's role here is structurally analogous.

A distinct surface-uniformity problem was observed in early generation trials. Even with varied personas and scenarios, generated dialogues exhibited convergent surface-level linguistic style: hedging and qualification patterns, filler density, turn-taking conventions, and repetition distribution remained similar across dialogues, attenuating the register variation present in naturalistic conflict corpora. Because surface style is independent of conflict psychology, this convergence is not corrected by persona variation alone — a character's TKI mode and activation trajectory shape when and why they conflict; they do not on their own constrain how they speak. Each persona additionally carries a *communicative register* specification — a character-level description of baseline stylistic features intrinsic to that persona, such as formality, emotional expressiveness, and preference for precision or hedging — which constrains surface consistency within a character across turns. This does not resolve cross-dialogue uniformity: characters with distinct registers still tend toward convergent surface distributions when produced by the same generator. To address this, each character is assigned a pragmatic stylistics profile, specified independently of persona psychology and conflict-handling mode. Each profile specifies four dimensions of surface behavior: silence and pause handling; filler frequency and character; turn-taking conventions, including openness to overlap; and patterns of hedging, qualification, and repetition. Six profiles are defined — from low-context direct (minimal hedging, intolerance of silence, clean turn handoffs) through high-context reflective, cooperative overlap, formal institutional, casual high-filler, and hierarchical deferential. Cross-assigning stylistics profiles independently of TKI modes and persona types produces pairings in which surface register varies orthogonally to deficit-relevant behavior, reducing cross-dialogue uniformity without constraining the conflict dynamics the persona design is meant to produce.

The scenario library consists of 20 conflict briefs, each defined along five fields: *conflict type* (the domain of the dispute); *relationship context* (the history, interdependence, and power differential of the parties); *surface issue* (the observable focal point of the disagreement); *party A brief*; and *party B brief* (the concrete risks, constraints, and underlying drivers for each party respectively). Scenarios were derived via RAG-assisted review of practitioner conflict resolution manuals, sampling broadly across conflict domains — workplace, family, commercial, neighborhood, institutional — to support corpus generalizability.

The first phase produces a gold standard: researcher-reviewed dialogues generated from persona-scenario pairs, annotated at the turn level using the schema defined above. Dialogue selection for the gold set is guided by three quality criteria drawn from the preference-based RL literature (Wirth et al., 2017): *generalizability* — persona-scenario pairings vary in conflict domain, party profile, relationship type, and power differential; *robustness* — pairings are selected to include boundary and adversarial cases in which deficit accumulation is ambiguous or multiple deficit types are simultaneously elevated; and *expressiveness* — preference pairs are constructed to discriminate meaningfully across the full intervention taxonomy, not merely to contrast clear successes with clear failures.

The second phase extends deficit labeling to the full corpus via Claude Sonnet-4-6, functioning here as a dedicated turn-level deficit annotator (mean κ = 0.671 against the gold standard) — a role distinct from the downstream reward model, which scores interventions rather than labeling deficits. Silver label quality is monitored through periodic comparison against the gold set, with consistency filtering applied to remove annotations that diverge from the established pattern beyond a set threshold.

A substantive limitation of the current corpus is that the dialogue generation pipeline has not been validated against naturalistic conflict data. Whether persona-scenario conditioned, two-agent LLM dialogues adequately represent the range and structure of real conflict interaction — specifically for the purposes of AARC deficit detection and mediation intervention evaluation — rests at present on design reasoning rather than empirical measurement. DiaSynth (Suresh et al., 2025) demonstrates that synthetic dialogues generated under analogous conditions capture 90.48% of the performance distribution of in-domain naturalistic data, but that result is domain-general and does not establish fitness for the specific task of deficit detection in mediation-relevant conflict. Establishing that fitness requires a dedicated empirical evaluation study: systematic comparison of the synthetic corpus against a reference set of naturalistic conflict transcripts, assessing whether the distribution of deficit signals, escalation trajectories, and stylistic variation in the synthetic data is consistent with what is observed in real interactions. Designing and conducting that study — including the collection or licensing of suitable real-world data — is deferred to future work.

### Gold/Silver Annotation

The annotation schema labels each dialogue turn independently for each of the four AARC components — deficit present or absent — using a trailing seven-turn context window that ends at the focal turn. Labels are assigned based on the observable signals specified in the preceding section, with preceding turns supplying the accumulating context on which several deficit signals depend; a turn may carry multiple deficit labels simultaneously. 

A central operational challenge in the annotation pipeline concerns the directionality of deficit attribution. Initial annotation trials using a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline to produce turn-level labels revealed a systematic confusion between deficit signals and repair attempts — particularly for Clarity and Reciprocity. The schema's speaker-attribution principle requires that a Clarity label marks the focal speaker as *proceeding on unverified epistemic ground*, not as articulating an uncertainty gap. An utterance such as "I'm not sure what we're talking about — I'd like to pin that down" describes a speaker naming an epistemic gap and orienting toward its resolution; it is a repair move, not a deficit. The deficit signal is structurally opposite: a speaker who treats an unverified interpretation as a settled premise and reasons forward from it without flagging the uncertainty. This inversion — between naming a gap and silently crossing it — proved difficult for both human and LLM annotators to track consistently under the initial schema.

The first IAA assessment, conducted under the v1 schema across 90 matched turns, yielded mean κ = 0.401 (A: 0.560, G: 0.352, R: 0.379, C: 0.313), with Clarity the weakest component at fair agreement. The confusion was symmetric: the human annotator and Claude Sonnet-4-6 showed the same characteristic error pattern, labeling repair-oriented turns — those in which a speaker explicitly acknowledges an unclear epistemic state and invites resolution — as deficit-positive. Subsequent refactoring of the schema to its current form introduced explicit repair resolution indicators, backward-looking discriminators for adjacent components, and worked gold standard examples covering both deficit and repair trajectories, resolving the principal confusion points at the cost of a substantially expanded annotation instrument.

Re-annotation of the gold set under the refined schema produced consistent improvement across all four components. Agreement stabilised across the final two annotation iterations (139 and 140 turns respectively) at mean κ = 0.671 (A: 0.767, G: 0.597, R: 0.789, C: 0.530), representing substantial agreement on Acknowledgement and Reciprocity and moderate agreement on Agency and Clarity — a net gain of +0.270 in mean κ over the v1 baseline, with the largest improvement on Clarity (+0.217), the component most susceptible to the repair/deficit inversion.

With a stable gold standard established, silver annotation scaling was attempted using a DSPy-optimized annotator compiled against a set of 15 hand-curated few-shot demonstrations drawn from the gold labels. An initial attempt using DSPy's BootstrapFewShot optimizer — which automatically selects demonstrations from gold traces based on a majority-component match criterion — produced annotation of insufficient quality for corpus scaling. Manual curation of demonstrations, selected to represent deficit-repair contrasts and boundary cases across all four components, improved agreement marginally but did not close the capability gap. Evaluation of the DSPy/GPT-4o configuration against 100 gold turns yielded mean κ = 0.391 (A: 0.463, G: 0.405, R: 0.420, C: 0.274) — approximating the v1-schema Sonnet baseline and falling well below the refined-schema ceiling. The Clarity component showed the lowest agreement (κ = 0.274) and a bidirectional error pattern consistent with residual repair/deficit confusion: despite an overall positive rate close to the gold baseline, the specific turns labeled are poorly calibrated, suggesting simultaneous mislabeling of repair turns as deficits and failure to detect actual deficit turns.

Several less expensive alternatives were evaluated before committing to a frontier model for the full silver pass. Smaller open-weight models available via local inference were rejected on capability grounds: the repair/deficit distinction requires tracking both the direction of epistemic action and the identity of the deficit-exhibiting speaker across a multi-turn window, and this reasoning consistently degraded below the quality threshold established by the GPT-4o evaluation. Computable proxy signals derived from FPO's friction functional — specifically NLI-based contradiction detection as a proxy for Clarity's self-contradiction signal — were considered as an annotation-free alternative, but provide no operationalization for Acknowledgement, Agency, or Reciprocity, which depend on intra-party psychological signals not reducible to the epistemic operations FPO targets. Parallel evaluation of Claude Sonnet-4-6 on the same 100-turn validation set yielded mean κ = 0.671, matching the human gold annotation ceiling and confirming that the capability gap between GPT-4o and Sonnet on this task is structural rather than prompt-dependent. Given the corpus scale of approximately 1,300 unannotated turns and the modest inference cost of a single frontier model pass, the silver annotation will proceed with Claude Sonnet-4-6 as the annotation model. Silver label quality will be monitored against the gold set by component using per-component F1 score, with particular attention to Clarity positive rates as the primary quality risk: the validation pass shows Sonnet labeling Clarity-positive on 21.4% of turns against a gold baseline of 15.7%, an over-labeling tendency that warrants spot-checking during the full pass.

A secondary concern with a Claude-only annotation pipeline is the risk of a closed preference loop: using the same model family for both corpus generation and silver annotation may encode model-specific stylistic and evaluative preferences as annotation signal rather than genuine mediation quality. To assess an alternative, GPT-5.4 was evaluated against the same 140-turn gold set using the raw AARC taxonomy prompt without optimization, yielding mean κ = 0.413 (A: 0.534, G: 0.389, R: 0.533, C: 0.196). While this falls below the Claude Sonnet ceiling, it clears the operational threshold for annotation use. Critically, cross-model agreement between Claude Sonnet-4-6 and GPT-5.4 is κ = 0.340, confirming that the two models produce genuinely divergent labelings rather than correlated errors — validating the diversity rationale. The primary quality gap is in Clarity, where GPT-5.4 over-labels at 25.0% against a gold baseline of 15.7%, the same direction as Claude's over-labeling tendency but more pronounced. DSPy MiPROv2 optimization was applied to the GPT-5.4 annotator using per-label mean accuracy as the optimization metric — a per-example proxy for minibatch-level macro-F1 — compiled against 100 training examples and evaluated on a 40-turn held-out val set. The unoptimized zero-shot GPT-5.4 baseline achieved macro-F1 = 0.419 on the val set (A: 0.588, G: 0.267, R: 0.600, C: 0.222). A light-budget MiPROv2 run (10 trials, 6 instruction candidates, 6 few-shot candidate sets) improved this to macro-F1 = 0.505 (A: 0.769, G: 0.400, R: 0.600, C: 0.250), a gain of +0.086. Acknowledgement showed the largest improvement (+0.181, precision improving to 1.000), and Agency improved substantially (+0.133). Clarity remains the weakest component (F1 = 0.250), with persistent low recall (0.200) consistent with the bidirectional repair/deficit confusion identified in earlier evaluations; the optimization did not resolve this pattern. A medium-budget MiPROv2 run (18 trials, 12 instruction candidates, 12 few-shot candidate sets) was subsequently attempted. The best trial score improved marginally to 88.75 (from 88.12), but the final optimized program produced a lower macro-F1 of 0.493 — a regression of −0.012 relative to the light run. Critically, the medium optimizer completely suppressed Clarity prediction: the best-scoring configuration predicted Clarity-absent for all val examples, yielding F1 = 0.000 on that component while improving Reciprocity to F1 = 0.857. This outcome identifies a structural vulnerability in the per-example accuracy metric: with Clarity positive in only 15.7% of turns, a model that always predicts Clarity-absent scores 84.3% label accuracy on that component, which is sufficient to inflate the aggregate metric. The optimizer exploited this imbalance, trading Clarity recall for gains elsewhere. In response, the optimization metric was revised to a recall-weighted formulation (true positive: 1.0; true negative: 0.3; false positive and false negative: 0.0), which directly penalises the all-absent strategy on low-prevalence labels without entirely discarding true-negative signal.

The optimization was subsequently restructured. Clarity was removed from the output signature entirely — excluded from both the annotator's prediction targets and the optimization metric — so that its definitional problems could not suppress gradient signal on the remaining components. A medium-budget MiPROv2 run using the recall-weighted metric (18 trials, 12 instruction candidates, val-size=70) was compiled against a three-label program predicting Acknowledgement, Agency, and Reciprocity. The unoptimized three-label baseline achieved macro-F1 = 0.436 on the 70-turn held-out val set (Ack: 0.650, G: 0.229, R: 0.429). The optimized program reached macro-F1 = 0.572 (Ack: 0.800, G: 0.250, R: 0.667), a gain of +0.137.

The results are component-selective. Acknowledgement reached F1 = 0.800 — a gain of +0.167 over the unoptimized GPT-5.4 full-set baseline and within 0.024 of the Claude Sonnet ceiling (0.824) — indicating that its definition is specified with sufficient behavioral precision for the optimizer to find a stable instruction. Reciprocity improved to F1 = 0.667, clearing the GPT-5.4 full-set baseline by +0.072. Agency, however, regressed: F1 = 0.250 against a GPT-5.4 unoptimized baseline of 0.435, produced by 21 false positives against 4 true positives. The recall-weighted metric was specifically designed to prevent all-absent suppression; Agency had sufficient positives in the training set to provide signal. Across 18 trials, the optimizer could not improve Agency precision beyond 0.160, indicating that the boundary between Agency deficit and general conflict distress is not specified with sufficient precision in the current taxonomy for a prompted model to locate it reliably. The pattern is structurally identical to the Clarity failure: the problem is definitional, not an artifact of metric design or search budget.

These results establish the limit of the DSPy/GPT-5.4 optimization track. Two of four AARC components — Clarity and Agency — cannot be reliably annotated at corpus scale without taxonomy revision: their boundary conditions are underspecified relative to the distributional properties of conflict dialogue. The two components that annotate well — Acknowledgement and Reciprocity — do so because their deficit signals are operationalized with behavioral specificity sufficient for a prompted model to generalize. GPT-5.4 with DSPy optimization does not meet the quality threshold for full corpus annotation; Claude Sonnet-4-6, at mean κ = 0.671 against human gold, remains the only annotator meeting that threshold, and the primary silver annotation pass will proceed accordingly.

A third annotator — Qwen 32B served via Groq — was also investigated to further broaden the annotation signal across model families. This was not pursued: the Groq developer plan imposes a 6,000 tokens-per-minute ceiling, which is structurally incompatible with the full taxonomy prompt (~16,000 tokens per call); a single annotation request exceeds the per-minute budget, making the 140-turn gold evaluation infeasible at the current service tier.

### Training and Evaluation

The annotation track established the conditions under which corpus-scale training can proceed, but not without revealing a structural constraint that bounds the training plan. Claude Sonnet-4-6, at mean κ = 0.671 against the human gold standard, meets the quality threshold for a full silver annotation pass across all four AARC components. That threshold is not uniform: Acknowledgement (κ = 0.767) and Reciprocity (κ = 0.789) annotate reliably, their deficit signals operationalized with behavioral specificity sufficient for a prompted model to generalize. Agency (κ = 0.597) and Clarity (κ = 0.530) present a more constrained picture. The DSPy/GPT-5.4 optimization track revealed that both components have underspecified boundary conditions — boundaries that optimization pressure cannot resolve because the problem is definitional rather than prompt-dependent. The silver annotation pass proceeds across all four components; the reward model trained on the resulting corpus is bounded accordingly. Its signal is strongest for Acknowledgement and Reciprocity; its generalizability on Agency and Clarity is limited pending taxonomy revision, a fresh gold annotation pass under the revised schema, and a future optimization run. The ablation study will be interpreted with this constraint in mind: it tests whether AARC deficit labels add discriminative value over the friction-only baseline, not whether the trained model fully captures all four components in their final form.

The forward plan proceeds from completed silver annotation through the following pipeline. Window-level deficit status is derived algorithmically: a seven-turn sliding window is designated deficit-present on a given component if at least one turn within it carries a positive label for that component, and the count of positive turns within the window constitutes the severity signal. Annotating at the turn level rather than the window level directly serves two purposes: it produces a finer-grained reward signal — the position and density of deficit turns within a window, rather than a single binary label — and it maintains consistency between the annotator's information set and the downstream model's inference-time information set, ensuring that no label is assigned on the basis of information a model would not possess when processing the conversation causally.

Windows in which at least one component is labeled deficit-present constitute candidate intervention points. At each such point, a group of N candidate interventions is generated, drawn from the component-appropriate intervention types defined in the companion taxonomy. Each intervention is scored across all four AARC components — whether it targets the detected deficit, and whether it avoids worsening components not currently in deficit — and an aggregate score is computed as the unweighted sum. The group is ranked by aggregate score, producing an ordered preference signal rather than a binary preference pair. Ranking N interventions rather than selecting between two captures preference intensity across the full taxonomy: a well-targeted intervention ranks clearly above a marginal one, and the ordering preserves that discrimination rather than collapsing it into a binary judgment. It also preserves the multi-component structure of the reward — a single-winner binary preference would obscure whether a preferred intervention succeeded on one AARC component or all four. Equal weighting across components is adopted at the aggregation step; whether the AARC diagnostic priority ordering warrants differential weighting is deferred to future work.

Pairwise preference pairs for reward model training are derived from the group rankings by sampling contrastive pairs across the ranked list, applying the Bradley-Terry preference model (Bradley & Terry, 1952) as operationalized in RLHF reward model training (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022). The corpus is designed to yield between 5,000 and 20,000 preference pairs; reward model agreement on a held-out subset reaching at least 70% is the quality threshold before training proceeds — agreement below this threshold indicates either annotation noise or an insufficiently specified rubric, and triggers schema-level revision rather than proceeding. Inter-annotator reliability for deficit labels will be assessed using Krippendorff's alpha across the four binary components (Artstein & Poesio, 2008); silver label quality against the gold standard is assessed using per-component F1. At policy training time, the scalar outputs of the trained reward model are compatible with the GRPO paradigm (DeepSeek-AI, 2025): the policy samples G candidate completions per prompt, the reward model scores each, and GRPO computes group-relative advantages — *(r_i − mean) / std* — over that scored group. These are distinct uses of group structure: annotation ranks N candidate interventions to produce reward model training data; GRPO groups G policy samples to produce the RL update signal.


## Limitations & Future Work

**Evaluation circularity and construct validity.** The training and evaluation design has a structural circularity that bounds the interpretability of its results. The reward model is trained on annotations derived from a single researcher's judgments applied to LLM-generated synthetic dialogues, and evaluated against those same judgments as ground truth. The IAA metrics reported — Cohen's κ, Krippendorff's α, per-component F1 — measure label consistency between annotators, not the validity of the AARC framework as a predictor of mediation effectiveness. Demonstrating that Claude Sonnet-4-6 agrees with the researcher's gold labels at mean κ = 0.671 establishes that the schema is consistently applicable, not that the labels it produces correspond to the psychological states the AARC model names, or that interventions scored highly by the resulting reward model would be judged effective by a practicing mediator. This limitation is structural to the proof-of-concept scope: it cannot be eliminated within the current design, but it establishes a ceiling on the claims the results can support. External validation — comparative judgments from practicing mediators on the quality of system-generated interventions, or behavioral outcome measures from participants in controlled conflict sessions — is required before claims about the framework's utility for real-world mediation assistance can be substantiated. That study constitutes the primary gap between computational utility and construct validity, and is deferred to future work.

**Corpus naturalistic validity.** The dialogue generation pipeline has not been empirically evaluated against naturalistic conflict data. Whether persona-scenario conditioned, two-agent LLM dialogues adequately represent the range and structure of real conflict interaction — specifically for the purposes of AARC deficit detection and mediation intervention evaluation — rests at present on design reasoning rather than empirical measurement. DiaSynth (Suresh et al., 2025) demonstrates that synthetic dialogues generated under analogous conditions capture 90.48% of the performance distribution of in-domain naturalistic data, but that result is domain-general and does not establish fitness for deficit detection in mediation-relevant conflict. Establishing that fitness requires a dedicated empirical evaluation study: systematic comparison of the synthetic corpus against a reference set of naturalistic conflict transcripts, assessing whether the distribution of deficit signals, escalation trajectories, and stylistic variation in the synthetic data is consistent with what is observed in real interactions. Designing and conducting that study — including the collection or licensing of suitable naturalistic conflict data — is deferred to future work.

**Corpus register scope.** The synthetic corpus is produced primarily through LLM generation. LLMs trained on existing text reproduce dominant communicative register patterns consistent with Western, educated, metropolitan norms. The annotation schema and gold standard dialogues implicitly encode these norms: deficit signals are operationalized in terms of linguistic behaviors that are legible within this register but may manifest differently, or carry different pragmatic weight, in other cultural contexts. The corpus is therefore appropriately understood as scoped to a specific communicative register; its utility for cross-cultural mediation contexts — including First Nations mediations, intercultural disputes, and conflicts operating under significantly different conventions of face, silence, and directness — has not been assessed. Future corpus work should include explicit stratification by communicative register and, where appropriate, consultation with practitioners from target cultural contexts before applying the annotation schema.

**AARC priority ordering and corpus structure.** The current methodology applies equal weighting to AARC components at the turn level and does not enforce the diagnostic priority ordering (Acknowledgement → Agency → Reciprocity → Clarity) in the corpus generation design. AARC's priority ordering implies that foundational deficits warrant attention before higher-order ones, and that content-level correction is productive only once relational and procedural preconditions are in place. Whether this ordering can emerge from turn-level training data — without being explicitly instantiated in the corpus structure — or requires trajectory-structured dialogues in which foundational deficits are addressed before Clarity work begins, is an open question. Turn-level equal weighting is the operationally tractable choice for a first training run; it does not, however, allow the resulting model to demonstrate that the priority ordering is learnable as an inductive bias. A future corpus design should include explicitly structured trajectories and test whether a model trained on that structure generalizes the ordering.

**Agency and Clarity annotation.** Two of four AARC components — Clarity and Agency — proved resistant to DSPy optimization, and for the same underlying reason: their boundary conditions are insufficiently specified for a prompted model to reliably distinguish the target signal from adjacent phenomena in conflict dialogue. Clarity's definitional problem was identified earlier in the pipeline: inter-annotator agreement against human gold stands at κ = 0.196 for the unoptimized GPT-5.4 annotator, and medium-budget MiPROv2 optimization suppressed Clarity prediction entirely in the best-scoring configuration, confirming that the failure is definitional rather than metric-related. Agency's problem was revealed by the final optimization pass: despite a recall-weighted metric specifically designed to prevent all-absent suppression, and despite sufficient positive-class examples in the training set, the optimizer could not improve Agency precision beyond 0.160 across 18 trials, regressing F1 from the GPT-5.4 unoptimized baseline of 0.435 to 0.250. The boundary between Agency deficit — perceived foreclosure on one's ability to influence the situation — and general conflict distress or negativity is not specified with sufficient behavioral precision in the current taxonomy. Both components require revision: clearer deficit boundary conditions, worked gold standard examples covering the deficit-versus-adjacent-phenomenon distinction, and a fresh human gold annotation pass under the revised schema before further optimization is warranted. The primary silver annotation pass using Claude Sonnet-4-6 (mean κ = 0.671) proceeds across all four components, as Claude's agreement on Agency (κ = 0.597) and Clarity (κ = 0.530) clears the operational threshold established for corpus scaling; the taxonomy limitation applies specifically to the DSPy/GPT-5.4 optimization track and to the generalizability of any reward model trained on the current annotation set.
