# Ting-Toomey, S. (1988). Intercultural conflict styles: A face-negotiation theory.

## Citation

Ting-Toomey, S. (1988). Intercultural conflict styles: A face-negotiation theory. In Y. Y. Kim & W. B. Gudykunst (Eds.), *Theories in Intercultural Communication* (pp. 213–235). Sage.

## Abstract

The researching of facework can be found in a wide range of disciplines such as anthropology, psychology, sociology, linguistics, business management, international diplomacy, and human communication studies, among others. The concept of face has been used to explain linguistic politeness rituals, apology acts, embarrassment episodes, requesting behaviors, rapport-building, and conflict interactions. The origin of the face negotiation theory stemmed from the author’s dissatisfaction with the mainstream interpersonal conflict communication literature in
the early 1980s. In the mainstream Western-based interpersonal and workplace conflict research literature, the twin ideologies of conflict confrontation and self-disclosure were strongly endorsed, and conflict tactfulness, avoidance style, and silence strategy were minimized. 

## Project Relevance

Proposes that conflict behavior is organized around face concerns — the management of one's own and others' public self-image. High self-face concern produces assertive, direct strategies; high other-face concern produces accommodation and avoidance. The theory can be interpreted as informing the Agency component of the AARC extension: when a party's constraint is overridden or ignored, this can be understood as an other-face threat — a denial of the party's standing as a competent decision-maker. Ting-Toomey's framework explains why identity-layer failures trigger defensive posturing that does not resolve through epistemic clarification alone, and why FPO's existing assertive actions (challenge, refuse) are insufficient tools for face-repair.
