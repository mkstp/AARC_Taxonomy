## Abstract

[PENDING — book; no publisher abstract. Verifiable summary from chapters 11–12: Bowlby introduces "internal working models" as cognitive-affective representations that a person constructs, on the basis of attachment experience, of (a) the self as worthy or unworthy of care and (b) attachment figures and others as reliable or unreliable in responding to needs. These paired models are argued to be carried forward as relatively stable schemas that shape expectations, appraisals, and emotional responses in later interpersonal situations, including situations of separation, threat, and conflict. The models are updated slowly and often operate outside awareness. Source: reconstructed from secondary literature; verify against library copy of the text or Bartholomew & Horowitz (1991), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 61, 226–244, which operationalizes the self × other structure into four adult attachment styles.]

## Project Relevance

Grounds the persona fields `beliefs_about_self` and `beliefs_about_others` as a psychologically principled pair rather than an ad hoc stylistic choice. Bowlby's formulation — two coordinated representations, one self-directed and one other-directed, that jointly mediate situational interpretation — is precisely the structure used to condition how a persona reads an ambiguous move by its counterpart. Whether a counterpart's silence is experienced as withdrawal, indifference, or restraint is not determined by the surface content of the turn but by the persona's working model of others. This gives the director-LLM a theoretically grounded target for authenticity checking: a persona's interpretive moves must remain consistent with its declared working models across the dialogue window. The self × other pairing also maps onto AARC's component boundaries — a negative model of self (unworthy, helpless) correlates with Agency deficits; a negative model of others (unreliable, hostile) correlates with Reciprocity deficits and triggers escalation. *Verify: library copy of Bowlby (1973), Basic Books.*
